How we structure the first ninety days of every engagement.

Published October 2026  ·  Updated October 2026  ·  By OrcaPulse Editorial

A field guide. The audits we run, the questions we ask, and the first deliverable that decides the next twelve months.

Dec 2025 11 min read By OrcaPulse
Essay

Key takeaways

Why the first 90 days are the whole engagement in miniature.

Everything about the next 12 months of a client-agency relationship is encoded in the first 90 days: the trust level, the pace, the measurement framework, the voice the agency uses, the problem being worked on, the senior partner's actual involvement, and the client's internal narrative about the agency. Fail the first 90 days and the renewal conversation is already lost.

Yet most agencies spend the first 90 days on "discovery", produce a thick strategy deck somewhere around day 70, and start "actual work" around day 90. This is backwards. By day 90 the client has already decided whether the agency is a strategic partner or a vendor, and the strategy deck arrives after the verdict.

The structure that works runs in parallel: audit, prove, strategise, operationalise. Each stage overlaps the next by two weeks, and the whole 90 days is designed to produce one visible, measurable outcome by day 42 — before the strategy deck exists.

Weeks 1-2: the audit — fast, focused, uncomfortable.

The audit is not a 50-tab-spreadsheet discovery exercise. It is a specific, time-boxed, forensic look at the three to five things that most likely explain the client's current performance situation. The format is a 20-page document, written in prose, not slide-bulleted, delivered on day 14.

The audit covers: current acquisition mix with honest cost-per-outcome attribution, the top three content and creative patterns that are working or failing, the operational speed of lead handling, the measurement framework the client is currently using (and where it is lying to them), and one or two deeply uncomfortable observations the client has not yet accepted internally.

The uncomfortable observations are the single most important part. If the audit does not surface at least one, the audit was polite and useless. Clients respect fast truth-telling in week two. They lose respect for an audit that reads like a brochure of praise.

Weeks 3-6: the proof deliverable — the entire relationship rests here.

The proof deliverable is one specific, scoped, measurable project that produces a visible outcome by day 42. The scope is deliberately narrow — a landing-page overhaul, a specific channel test, a single campaign re-launch, an operational workflow fix. Not a strategy. An execution.

The purpose is simple: to prove, in the first 42 days, that the agency can produce an outcome the client's internal team was not producing. The specificity is the point. "We rebuilt the mobile booking flow and lifted conversion 22%" by day 42 is worth more than any strategy document produced in month three. The client has now seen the agency ship. Everything after this is easier.

The agencies that get this right do it on scope they fully control, with success criteria agreed in writing in week three, measured transparently against a pre-engagement baseline. The agencies that get it wrong try to do too much, miss the day-42 deadline, and spend the next six weeks explaining.

The strategy document is the easy part. The proof deliverable on day 42 is the whole relationship — ship it and the next 12 months are already won.

Weeks 7-10: the strategy document — one page, lived with.

By day 50-55 the audit is complete, the proof deliverable has shipped and been measured, and the agency has actually operated inside the client's business for seven weeks. This is the point at which a serious strategy document becomes possible. Before this point, any strategy document is theoretical.

The target length is one page. One. Not a 60-slide capabilities deck, not a 40-page strategic review. One page the client can tape to a wall, read in 60 seconds, and remember in month 11. The one page covers: the one dominant problem being worked on, the three channels or motions that will move it, the measurement framework, the operating rhythm, and the one thing the agency will not do.

Behind the one page is a longer working document, 20-30 pages, that holds the reasoning. The one page is the artefact the client lives with; the 30 pages are the engine room. The one page is what makes the strategy survive the next executive turnover inside the client's company.

Weeks 11-13: the operating rhythm handover.

The last three weeks of the first 90 days are about building the operating cadence that will carry the next 12 months. Monthly reviews, quarterly strategic sessions, named points of contact on both sides, the shared workspace, the measurement dashboard, the escalation path, the renewal-conversation calendar.

The named points of contact matter. Both sides. If the agency's senior partner is in the first 90 days and vanishes in month four, the client will notice and the relationship will decay. If the client's primary stakeholder leaves and no secondary stakeholder has been built, the account becomes vulnerable. The 90-day handover is where this is set up.

By day 90, the client should feel: audited, proven, strategised, operationalised. The retainer is in its working rhythm. The next quarterly review is on the calendar. The one-page strategy is on the wall. Everything that will happen in months 4-12 is already set in the shape of the first 90 days.

Common first-90-day failures that break the retainer
  • A "discovery" phase that runs to day 60 and produces no shippable outcome.
  • A thick strategy deck as the first visible deliverable — the client reads it, approves it, and quietly waits for execution that is now behind schedule.
  • No agreed success criteria for the proof deliverable in writing in week 3 — leading to a "was that a win or not?" argument on day 50.
  • Senior partner heavily involved weeks 1-6 and absent after. The client's internal perception of the agency's seniority collapses by month three.
  • No formal measurement baseline before the proof deliverable ships — making the day-42 result unmeasurable and unarguably ambiguous.

What a healthy day-90 looks like inside the client.

The client's internal narrative about the agency at day 90 is the single best predictor of renewal in month 12. If the client's CMO can say, in week 13, "they audited us honestly, they shipped something visible by week six, we agreed on a one-page strategy, and we are now in an operating rhythm" — the renewal is 80% done nine months before the conversation.

If the client's CMO says, at day 90, "they are still discovering, the strategy deck is good but we haven't seen them execute yet, and I'm not sure who to call" — the renewal is 80% lost nine months before the conversation. The arithmetic is unforgiving. The first 90 days are the whole year.

  1. Audit by day 14. 20-page prose document, 3-5 forensic observations, at least one uncomfortable truth.
  2. Scope the proof deliverable in writing by day 21. One specific, measurable, 42-day deliverable with pre-agreed success criteria.
  3. Ship the proof deliverable by day 42. With measurement against baseline. This is the non-negotiable milestone.
  4. Write the one-page strategy by day 70. Not 60 slides. One page. Living document for the whole year.
  5. Hand over the operating rhythm by day 90. Named contacts, cadence, dashboard, renewal calendar. The year is now set in motion.
