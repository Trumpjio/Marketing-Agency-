Pricing a premium agency: a defence of flat fees.

Published October 2026  ·  Updated October 2026  ·  By OrcaPulse Editorial

Hourly billing is a tax on honesty. Flat quarterly retainers are not a pricing choice they are a positioning choice.

Feb 2026 7 min read By OrcaPulse
Essay
Key takeaways
  • Flat quarterly retainers are a positioning choice, not just a pricing choice — they signal confidence in judgment-based delivery.
  • Hour-based billing selects for the wrong clients, the wrong work, and the wrong internal incentives in a specialist agency.
  • The three alternatives — hourly, FTE-based, performance-fee — each have specific failure modes that flat quarterly retainers avoid.
  • The transition requires discipline on scope and ruthlessness on client fit; the upside is dramatically better margin and dramatically simpler operations.
On this page
  1. Why hourly billing is a bad fit for specialist agency work.
  2. The flat retainer as a positioning statement.
  3. The three alternative models — and their specific failure modes.

Why hourly billing is a bad fit for specialist agency work.

Hourly billing is a sensible model for commodity work where the output is directly proportional to time spent — paralegal work, bookkeeping, mechanical copy production. It is a catastrophic model for judgment-based work where the output is proportional to the quality of thinking, not the hours logged.

The structural failures are predictable. Hourly billing rewards agencies for inefficiency — fast work is punished by lower fees. It penalises senior judgment — a 20-year CMO's hour is billed at 2-3x a junior's, so the junior does the work and the quality drops. It attracts cost-sensitive clients who negotiate hour rates and scrutinise timesheets, which is a terrible match for the strategic work a specialist should be selling.

The agencies that stay on hourly billing find themselves in a slow drift toward commodity work, mid-market clients, and juniorised delivery. The margin compresses, the quality drops, and the brand positions itself as a time-rental shop. Eventually the agency competes with offshore delivery firms and the game is over.

The flat retainer as a positioning statement.

A flat quarterly retainer — "$30,000 per quarter, this is the scope, this is the output, this is the measurement" — signals three things loudly before any delivery has started.

Confidence in judgment. The agency believes it will deliver outcomes worth the fee without needing to measure hours. The client is paying for a result, not a timesheet.

Alignment with senior time. There is no incentive to push the work to juniors. Senior partners do senior work because the fee is already paid.

Selection of serious clients. Clients comfortable with flat retainers are comfortable with judgment-based buying. Clients who insist on hourly rates and timesheets are a different buyer, and almost always the wrong fit for specialist work.

Flat quarterly retainers do not solve a pricing problem. They solve a positioning problem that was being miscounted as a pricing problem.

The three alternative models — and their specific failure modes.

FTE-based retainers ("we charge for 1.5 FTEs at $15k each per month") sound more rigorous than they are. They re-introduce timesheet-adjacent accounting through the side door, create an unresolvable fight about "which people on your team count as the FTEs", and incentivise padding the delivery team to defend the headcount. They are hourly billing with a monthly cap.

Performance-fee models ("5% of attributable revenue uplift") are seductive and almost always a trap. Attribution is a mess, the fee is tied to variables the agency does not control (product-market fit, sales execution, macro conditions), and the client-agency relationship becomes an attribution argument. Performance fees work only in narrow, measurable, high-trust scopes.

Project-based pricing ("$80k for the brand refresh, done in 10 weeks") is excellent for well-scoped one-off deliverables and bad for ongoing strategic engagements. It also exposes the agency to scope creep without a mechanism to charge for it.

The flat quarterly retainer avoids each of these failure modes. It assumes a long-term engagement, prices for judgment, and lets scope evolve naturally within a defined envelope.

The discipline required — scope and client fit.

Scope discipline. A flat retainer requires a crisp written scope and a crisp written out-of-scope list. "We advise on acquisition strategy, run paid-media planning, and deliver monthly performance reviews" is a scope. "We also build landing pages, produce creative, and sometimes do PR" without a defined envelope is where flat retainers go to die.

Client fit discipline. A flat retainer is a bad fit for a client who wants a timesheet, a client who treats the agency as an extended labour team, or a client who has not committed to a 12-month engagement. Saying no to these clients is non-negotiable. The agencies that transition to flat retainers successfully say no to roughly a third of their inbound in the first year.

Signs a flat retainer is about to fail
  • The client is asking for weekly timesheet-equivalent reports ("how many hours did Omar spend this week?").
  • The scope has drifted and nobody has written down what is now in and out — scope creep inside flat retainers is silent and corrosive.
  • The named partners are no longer the lead delivery team on the account — juniors have taken over and the retainer is being "managed" by the junior staff.
  • The client is comparing fees to internal FTE costs or hourly rate-cards from competitors — the positioning conversation was lost.
  • The agency internally has started counting hours against the retainer. The moment this happens, the model has collapsed inside the agency, regardless of what the invoice says.

The transition — how to move an existing book from hourly to flat.

Transitioning an existing client book is uncomfortable but mechanical. For each account, write a flat quarterly scope that approximates the current average quarterly fee. Present it as an upgrade — "no more surprise invoices, no more hour-tracking disputes, one price per quarter, same work." Most clients prefer the predictability.

Clients who refuse the model self-select out, and this is useful information. Those are the clients who would have been a problem on flat retainers anyway. Let them leave. The agency that emerges on the other side of this transition is simpler, more profitable, and selling a different thing to a different market.

The transition takes 9-18 months in practice. Half the book moves cleanly, a quarter moves after one iteration, and a quarter leaves. The remaining three-quarters pay more per account than the full previous book did, with less operational overhead. The arithmetic is unforgiving in favour of the shift.

  1. Write the scope and the out-of-scope list. One page. In and out of scope. What is reported, when, how.
  2. Price the retainer at your true senior rate. Not an hourly conversion. A judgment-fee. Specialists in your category are a market reference, not junior labour costs.
  3. Introduce it to existing clients as an upgrade. Not a renegotiation. "One price, no surprises, same senior team."
  4. Say no to clients who want timesheets. Mandatory. Every exception corrodes the whole book.
  5. Audit the retainer every quarter. Scope drift is silent. If delivery has moved outside the envelope, re-price or re-scope. Do not absorb it.
